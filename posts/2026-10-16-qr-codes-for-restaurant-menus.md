A QR code on the table lets guests open your menu on their own phones. It saves printing costs, lets you change prices without reprinting everything and suits guests who want to read the menu in their own time. Done badly, it is a frustration. Done well, it takes an afternoon. Here is how to do it well.

## Step 1: put the menu on a page that works on phones

The QR code only opens a web address, so the menu must already be online. Whatever you choose, check that it:

- loads quickly on mobile data, not only on Wi-Fi;
- is readable without zooming and without pinching;
- does not need an account or an app;
- does not force guests to download a large PDF with tiny type.

A simple web page with headings for starters, mains and drinks usually works better than a scanned photo of a printed menu. If you use a PDF, keep it small and set at a readable size for a phone screen.

## Step 2: shorten the address

Menu addresses are often long. Paste yours into the [URL shortener](/) and give it a readable ending such as `lunch_menu`. The short link makes the QR code simpler, which helps it scan in dim restaurant light. It also gives you something to print under the code for guests who prefer to type.

## Step 3: make the code

Open the [QR code generator](/qr-code-generator/), paste the short link and download the code. For printing, choose the SVG, or the largest PNG. Keep the colours dark on light, because a code on a coloured background is harder to read in low light. Leave the white border around the code untouched.

## Step 4: pick the right size

A code that will be scanned from a few centimetres, on a table card, can be about 3 to 4 cm wide. A code on a window or a poster that people scan from further away needs to be bigger. A rule of thumb is that the code should be about one tenth of the distance it will be scanned from. A window sign read from two metres away should be at least 20 cm wide.

## Step 5: place it where guests will use it

- **On the table**, on a stand or printed card at an angle, not flat under a glass.
- **At the door or counter** for guests who order there.
- **Near good lighting**, because glare from a candle or lamp can ruin a scan.
- **On a surface that wipes clean**, because menus get sticky. Laminated or sealed cards survive cleaning.

Add a short line of text next to the code, such as "Scan to see the menu". Many guests still wonder whether it is a menu or an advertisement.

## Step 6: test before you print 100 copies

Print one copy and scan it with at least three different phones, ideally an older one too. Do it in the room, at the table, under the real light. Check that the page opens quickly and that the menu looks right. Only then print the full set.

## Step 7: keep a plan B

Not every guest will want to scan. Keep a few paper menus for people without a smartphone or with a flat battery, and for guests who simply prefer paper. It is also kind to older guests, and costs little.

## Keeping the menu up to date

Changing the menu is the point of a QR menu. If you keep the same web page address and edit the page, the printed code keeps working. If the address must change, make a new short link and reprint. Because a short link cannot be edited, plan to keep the menu page at a stable address.

## Common mistakes

- A code printed too small for the distance.
- A code stretched or squashed to fit a design.
- Colours that look stylish but have low contrast.
- A menu page that takes ten seconds to load.
- Printing before testing.
