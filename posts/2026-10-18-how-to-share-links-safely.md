Links are how we share almost everything online, and they are also how most scams reach people. You do not need technical knowledge to stay safe. A handful of habits, applied every time, remove most of the risk. This guide covers both sides: what to do when you send a link, and what to do when you receive one.

## When you send a link

**Check where it goes.** Open the link yourself before you share it. It sounds obvious, but a mistyped address or a page that has been removed is the most common reason a shared link fails.

**Do not share private pages.** If a page shows personal data, a private document or an account, anyone with the address may be able to open it. A short link does not make a page private. It only makes the address shorter. Use the sharing settings of the service that holds the file, and share only what you intend others to see.

**Remove tracking junk if you can.** Links copied from shops and search results often include long strings after a question mark. Those strings can reveal where you were browsing. If you only want to share the page, trim the address to the part before the question mark, test it and then share.

**Say what the link is.** A message that says only "look at this" with a link is how scams work. Give a sentence of context, such as "Here is the booking page for Saturday." People are much more willing to trust a link that comes with a reason.

**Use a short link carefully.** A short link is useful in messages, on slides and in print, but hides the destination. Give it a descriptive custom ending, and let people know they can preview it. See our guide on [choosing a custom ending](/blog/how-to-choose-a-custom-short-link-ending/).

## When you receive a link

**Pause when you feel hurried.** Scams rely on pressure: a parcel you must claim, an account that will close in an hour, a prize that expires. Real organisations rarely demand immediate action through a link.

**Look at the domain.** The part between `https://` and the first single slash is the real destination. Check it carefully. `paypal.com.account-check.example` belongs to `account-check.example`, not to PayPal. Misspellings such as `arnazon` for `amazon` are another common trick.

**Preview short links.** Add a plus sign (`+`) to the end of any `urlshort.tr` link to see the full destination before you open it, or paste it into the [link checker](/check-short-link/). For other services, look for their own preview option. Our guide on [checking where a short link goes](/blog/are-short-links-safe/) explains this in detail.

**Go to the website yourself.** If a message claims to come from your bank or a delivery company, do not use its link. Type the organisation's address into your browser or use its official app.

**Be careful with QR codes.** A QR code is just a link in picture form. A sticker on a public poster may have been placed over the original one. Check the address your phone shows before you open it, and never enter passwords or payment details after scanning a code you cannot verify.

**Keep your software updated.** Browsers and phones update to block known dangerous sites. Updates are one of the best protections you have.

## What to do if you clicked something suspicious

1. Close the page. Do not enter any information.
2. If you did enter a password, change it at once on the real site, and on any other site where you used the same one.
3. If you entered payment details, contact your bank or card provider.
4. Run a security scan on your device if you downloaded anything.
5. Turn on two-step sign-in for important accounts.

## A five-second check

Before you open any link you did not expect, ask yourself:

- Do I know who sent it?
- Does the domain look right?
- Is there pressure to act quickly?
- Can I reach the same page another way?

If any answer worries you, do not open it. You can [shorten and share your own links](/) with a preview available to everyone who receives them.
